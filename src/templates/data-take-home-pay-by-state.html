What the numbers actually show

{{ANALYSIS_BLOCKS}}

A bigger paycheck is not the same as being better off

This study measures one thing: the dollars that reach your bank account from a {{SALARY}} salary. It says nothing about what those dollars buy. Cost of living is not modelled here at all, and it routinely swamps the {{SPREAD}} spread above. Housing is the largest single household expense in most budgets and can differ by far more than that between two states in the same row of this table.

States with no income tax still have to raise revenue somewhere, and they generally do it through sales and property taxes instead. Here are the states at the top and the bottom of the take-home ranking, next to the two taxes a paycheck calculator structurally cannot see:

How we computed this

Every figure on this page is computed at build time by the same open calculation engine that powers the {{ROW_COUNT}} state paycheck calculators on this site. Nothing here is typed in by hand, so when a state publishes new brackets, this page changes with it.

The inputs, held constant
{{SALARY}} gross salary, single filing status, no dependents, paid as one annual amount, no pre-tax deductions, {{TAX_YEAR}} tax rules. Only the state changes between rows.
Federal income tax
{{TAX_YEAR}} federal brackets applied to gross pay less the {{FED_STD_DED}} single standard deduction, per IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32. No credits, no itemizing, no W-4 adjustments.
FICA
Social Security at 6.2% on wages up to the {{TAX_YEAR}} wage base of {{SS_WAGE_BASE}}, and Medicare at 1.45% with no cap, per the SSA {{TAX_YEAR}} COLA fact sheet. {{SALARY}} is below the Additional Medicare threshold, so that 0.9% surtax does not apply here.
State income tax
Each state's own {{TAX_YEAR}} rate structure, flat or graduated, applied after that state's own standard deduction where it offers one. {{NOTAX_COUNT}} states levy no wage income tax at all: {{NOTAX_STATES}}.
State payroll deductions
Employee-side disability and paid-leave premiums in the {{PROGRAM_COUNT}} jurisdictions that have them, with each program's own rate, wage base and annual cap: {{PROGRAM_STATES}}. These are withheld post-tax on gross wages, so they reduce take-home pay without reducing taxable income.
Rounding and ties
Dollar figures are rounded to the nearest dollar for display; ranks are computed on the unrounded values, and tied states share a rank.

What this does not include

A clean comparison needs identical inputs, which means leaving real things out. The figures above will not match your pay stub, and here is exactly why:

  • Local income taxes. {{LOCAL_COUNT}} states allow city, county or school-district income taxes that are not modelled here: {{LOCAL_STATES}}. In practice these can move take-home pay by hundreds or thousands of dollars, and they push a few states further down than this table shows.
  • Pre-tax deductions. No 401(k), no HSA or FSA, no health premiums. Those reduce taxable income, so a real paycheck in a high-tax state closes some of the gap shown here.
  • Credits, itemizing and other filing statuses. Single filers taking the standard deduction only. Married filing jointly, head of household, dependents and state-specific credits all change the answer, sometimes substantially.
  • Non-wage income and employer-side taxes. Investment income, self-employment income and the employer's half of FICA are all out of scope.
  • Cost of living. As set out above, not modelled, and usually larger than the entire {{SPREAD}} spread.
