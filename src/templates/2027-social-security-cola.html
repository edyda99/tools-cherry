How the COLA is actually calculated

  • The index is CPI-W, not the headline inflation number you hear on the news. CPI-W tracks prices for urban wage earners and clerical workers, a different basket from the CPI-U figure usually reported.
  • The comparison is quarter to quarter: the July–September average of this year against the July–September average of the last year a COLA was set. Not December to December, not a twelve-month average.
  • It is rounded to the nearest tenth of a percent. A raw 3.84% becomes 3.8%; 3.86% becomes 3.9%.
  • It can never be negative. If prices fall, the COLA is 0.0% and benefits stay where they are. Benefits are not cut.
  • Each person's new monthly amount is rounded down to the whole dollar, which is why the calculator above will not show you an exact percentage of your benefit.
  • Announced mid-October, paid from January. The announcement normally comes the same morning the September price data is published.

The catch nobody puts in the headline: Medicare Part B

If your Medicare Part B premium comes out of your Social Security payment — for most people it does — then the amount that actually reaches your bank account goes up by less than the COLA suggests, because the Part B premium for 2027 rises too. That premium is set separately, by a different agency, and is normally announced in November, a month after the COLA. So in October you will know your increase and not yet know how much of it you keep. We are not going to compute that for you with a premium nobody has published; when the figure exists it is a straight subtraction from the monthly increase shown above.
