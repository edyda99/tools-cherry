Where the numbers come from

Nothing on this page is typed in by hand. Every figure is calculated when the site is built, by the same engine that runs the {{ROW_COUNT}} state paycheck calculators, so the table cannot drift away from the calculators and it updates itself when a state publishes new tax brackets.

Federal income tax uses the {{TAX_YEAR}} brackets on your pay after the {{FED_STD_DED}} single standard deduction. Social Security is 6.2% up to the {{TAX_YEAR}} wage base of {{SS_WAGE_BASE}}, and Medicare is 1.45% with no ceiling. State income tax uses each state's own {{TAX_YEAR}} rates and its own standard deduction where it has one. {{NOTAX_COUNT}} states charge no income tax on wages at all: {{NOTAX_STATES}}.

What this will not tell you

One salary, one filing status, one set of assumptions. That is what makes the states comparable, and it is also why these figures will not match your own pay stub. Here is what is missing:

  • Your deductions. Money you put into a 401(k), an HSA or health insurance comes out before tax, which lowers the tax bill and changes every number above.
  • Your filing status and family. Married filing jointly, head of household, children and state credits all move the answer, sometimes by thousands.
  • City and county income taxes. {{LOCAL_COUNT}} states let a city, county or school district charge income tax on top of the state's. Those are not in this table: {{LOCAL_STATES}}.
  • What the money buys. Cost of living is not modelled here at all, and rent alone can easily be worth more than the {{SPREAD}} spread. A bigger paycheck is not the same thing as being better off.

For a figure that is actually about you, open your own state's calculator, put in your real salary and filing status, and use the advanced questions for your deductions: all {{ROW_COUNT}} state paycheck calculators.
